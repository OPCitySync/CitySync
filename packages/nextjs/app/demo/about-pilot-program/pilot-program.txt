The City/Sync Pilot Framework
A Deployment Model for Programmable Public Coordination
The City/Sync Pilot Framework establishes a deployment model through which cities can onboard into programmable public coordination infrastructure. Its purpose is to demonstrate how institutional authority and structured civic participation can operate within a unified administrative environment while remaining governable and replicable.
The pilot is designed to produce operational precedent. It introduces the procedural, economic, social, and governance conditions required for cities to coordinate participation as a functional component of public service ecosystems rather than as an informal or peripheral activity.
The purpose of this document is to outline the design logic, deployment structure, governance model, and evaluation criteria that guide the pilot. Its role is to define a working model that other jurisdictions can assess and implement.
This proposed framework seeks to implement a pilot across two distinct governance contexts, one in Berkeley, California and another in Mexico City, Mexico.  The goal is to validate that programmable coordination can be adopted within varied institutional environments without requiring structural disruption to existing administrative systems.
Four objectives guide the deployment:
	•	Establish a repeatable municipal onboarding process. (Procedural)
	•	Validate participation issuance and service redemption mechanisms and processes. (Economic)
	•	Validate the concept of Mass Coordination Events (MCE) as a method for bootstrapping local administration. (Social)
	•	Expand the issuer and redeemer ecosystem through real-world operational use. (Governance)
Together, these objectives support a larger outcome that makes programmable public coordination observable and adoptable by cities.
 Procedural Implementation  In order to establish a repeatable municipal onboarding process, we need to understand who the potential actors working within this network are and what possible roles they can play within the network.  
Roles 
Issuers - Organizations that issue tasks that expand their non-private goods and services mission.
Eligibility Requirements: Incorporated public-service organizations that have a local service history, and have the capacity to manage a volunteer program. Issuers must commit to the adherence of governance mandates from representative Issuer organizations. 
Redeemers - Organizations that offer access to goods and services.
Eligibility Requirements: Incorporated Public or Private organizations that are willing to redeem access to goods or services with Civic Credits. Redeemers must commit to the adherence of governance mandates from representative Redeemer organizations.
Civic Participants - Individuals who claim and execute civic-labor opportunities provided by Issuers.
Eligibility Requirements: Any citizen/non-citizen who is capable of performing tasks offered by local Issuer organizations.  Participants must commit to the adherence of governance mandates from representative Issuer and Redeemer organizations.
Validators - Individuals or Organizations that verify the execution of civic tasks.
Eligibility Requirements: Individuals or organizations who are capable of verifying the work performed by Civic Participants.  Validators must commit to the adherence of governance mandates from their reporting Issuers organization.
The framework for these pilots is intentionally procedural. It is structured so that participating jurisdictions can demonstrate that the model can function, and that it can be reproduced. The pilot operates as guidance infrastructure that establishes the conditions under which a new coordination layer becomes administratively viable.
Deployment Models as Instruments of Administrative Change
Institutional change becomes durable only when new operating patterns are introduced under real conditions, observed, and refined through practice. For this reason, deployment models function as critical instruments of administrative evolution.
Historically, major shifts in administrative governance have followed this pattern. New procurement structures, digital service platforms, participatory budgeting mechanisms, and integrated data systems scaled because early jurisdictions demonstrated that they could operate within the constraints of law, oversight, budgeting, and public accountability.
The introduction of programmable coordination presents a similar environment. While the technical capacity to structure participation has advanced significantly, public institutions require operational pathways that translate capability into practiced governance. Without a deployment model, programmable coordination remains conceptual.
The City/Sync Pilot Framework is designed to provide that pathway.
Rather than positioning programmable coordination as a disruptive alternative to existing administrative systems, the framework introduces it procedurally. Administrative systems evolve most effectively when new operational layers integrate with established governance structures rather than attempting to replace them.
Deployment models serve two functions. Internally, they allow participating jurisdictions to evaluate feasibility, governance requirements, and institutional fit. Externally, they generate observable precedent that allows peer jurisdictions to assess whether an emerging coordination pattern is administratively viable.
Precedent plays a decisive role in public-sector adoption. Cities rarely move towards new systems, and when they do, it’s because the risks of innovation have been reduced by the demonstrated experience of others. A well-structured pilot should lower the psychological and political barriers that often slow institutional change.
The Pilot Framework success should be measured by operational performance and its ability to establish a recognizable administrative pattern that other jurisdictions can confidently follow.

Objectives of the City/Sync Pilot
The City/Sync Pilot is structured to establish the operational conditions under which programmable public coordination can function as an adoptable administrative layer. Its objectives are procedural rather than experimental, focusing on the mechanics required for institutional integration and long-term replication.
The first objective is to establish a repeatable municipal onboarding process. Participating jurisdictions must be able to integrate coordination infrastructure without requiring structural reorganization or exceptional governance accommodations. The pilot evaluates how cities can introduce programmable coordination through existing administrative pathways, ensuring that adoption is perceived as an extension of institutional capability rather than a departure from it.  Issuers and Redeemers within this system need to integrate the coordination layers as a natural extension of already acceptable practices.  For Issuers, this means working with organizations who already facilitate volunteer programs and can issue tasks and verify tasks as part of their natural routines.  For Redeemers, integrating the redemption of civic-credits should follow a predictable process that follows the same conditions for existing point-of-sale systems. 
The second objective is to validate the mechanics of participation issuance and service redemption within real service environments. Civic participation becomes operationally meaningful only when contributions can be recognized, accounted for, and exchanged through pathways that remain politically legible and administratively manageable. The pilot will examine how issuance models align with nonprofit and public-sector program goals, and how redemption can occur without introducing budgetary ambiguity or regulatory friction.
The third objective is to validate the concept of Mass Coordination Events (MCE).  These are time-bound events that center all issuer tasks around a collective communal goal (clearing a local government backlog, a cleanup of an area of a city, the identification and reporting of public safety hazards in a neighborhood, etc.).  MCE’s serve two purposes: (1) to show what’s possible when a city comes to consensus on a collective mission and executes on that mission using this new coordination layer, and (2) to bootstrap participants, issuers, and redeemers into the coordination network.
The fourth objective is to expand the issuer and redeemer ecosystem through active deployment. A coordination layer gains durability when institutions beyond the originating participants recognize clear incentives to join. By generating measurable impact for early issuers and establishing credible redemption channels, the pilot is designed to attract additional organizations, gradually increasing the density and utility of the coordination network.
Together, these objectives make programmable public coordination observable within functioning administrative contexts. Observability is essential for institutional adoption. When coordination mechanisms can be seen operating by increasing civic participation, supporting organizations in the provision of their services, and are transparent in their governance processes, they can transition from conceptual possibility to a legitimate administrative option.
The pilot is designed to confirm that the model can function, and to demonstrate how it functions under the conditions that define public governance. 
Cross-Governance Deployment
The proposed City/Sync Pilot is implemented concurrently across two distinct urban governance environments: Berkeley, California and Mexico City, Mexico. This dual-context deployment is intentional. For a coordination layer to be considered administratively viable, it must demonstrate adaptability across varied institutional conditions rather than functioning only within a single regulatory or cultural setting.
Berkeley represents a governance environment characterized by civic engagement, policy experimentation, and a strong tradition of public-sector innovation. Administrative systems in such contexts often possess both the institutional flexibility and community participation necessary to evaluate emerging coordination models under supportive conditions.
Mexico City presents a different operational landscape. As one of the largest metropolitan areas in the world, it operates at a scale and complexity that places significant demands on service coordination. Institutional processes must function across dense populations with varied economic realities, and highly distributed service needs. Demonstrating programmable coordination within such an environment signals that the model can operate beyond small-city conditions.
The objective is not to compare outcomes between cities, but to observe whether a shared coordination framework can integrate with distinct administrative structures while maintaining operational coherence.
Cross-governance deployment also reduces perceived adoption risk for observing jurisdictions. When an administrative model operates across varied contexts, it becomes more difficult to classify it as locally idiosyncratic.
This is a critical characteristic for any administrative pattern seeking broader adoption. Cities evaluating the model must be able to envision integration within their own governance frameworks. Cross-context operation provides that assurance by illustrating that programmable coordination is not bound to a singular regulatory philosophy or municipal scale.
The Dual-Market Coordination Engine
The City/Sync Pilot functions through the interaction of two interdependent participation markets: the issuer ecosystem and the participant community. The stability of the coordination layer depends not on the growth of one side alone, but on the balanced expansion of both.
Issuers include nonprofit organizations, public agencies, and community institutions.  They introduce structured participation opportunities aligned with measurable service objectives. These entities define the activities through which civic contribution becomes operationally meaningful, translating organizational goals into accessible pathways for citizen engagement.
Participants supply the civic capacity that activates these pathways. Their contributions generate the activity through which programs expand reach, services increase responsiveness, and institutional impact deepens. Participation will follow the presence of clearly structured opportunities. For this reason, the pilot treats issuer onboarding as the primary driver of coordination density.
Redemption entities complete the operational cycle by providing service pathways through which participation is recognized and exchanged. Politically legible redemption is essential for this type of pilot to function. When participants can translate contribution into access through public services, nonprofit programs, or community resources, the coordination layer gains behavioral credibility. Contribution becomes symbolic and functionally integrated into service ecosystems.
The interaction of issuance, participation, and redemption forms a self-reinforcing coordination loop. As issuers observe increased engagement, the incentive to introduce additional participation pathways strengthens. Expanded opportunities attract broader participant involvement, which in turn encourages additional redemption partnerships as service providers recognize the value of an activated civic base.
This dynamic produces what may be understood as coordination momentum. Rather than relying on continuous external stimulus, the system begins to generate its own expansion through the mutually reinforcing incentives of participating institutions and residents.
Designing the pilot as a dual-market environment also creates systemic durability. Civic initiatives often falter when participation outpaces institutional capacity, or when organizations offer programs without sufficient engagement. By structuring growth around both issuer expansion and participant activation, the framework reduces the likelihood of such imbalance.
Importantly, the dual-market model positions the coordination layer as infrastructure rather than as a standalone program. When organizations begin planning around participation pathways, and when residents anticipate their availability, the coordination layer transitions from initiative to administrative capability.
The pilot measures success through participation metrics, repeat issuer engagement, expanding redemption pathways, and increasing predictability in participation flows. These indicators would suggest that the coordination layer is becoming self-supporting, which is a critical threshold for long-term institutional adoption.

Issuer Onboarding as Infrastructure
The durability of the City/Sync coordination layer depends on the systematic onboarding of issuers. Issuers are not auxiliary participants in the pilot; they are the institutional interfaces through which civic participation becomes operational. Without a dense and expanding issuer ecosystem, programmable coordination remains abstract. With one, it becomes administratively useful.
For this reason, issuer onboarding is treated as infrastructure rather than outreach. The pilot does not rely on ad hoc recruitment or informal partnerships. Instead, it establishes a repeatable process through which nonprofit organizations, public agencies, and civic institutions can integrate their objectives into the coordination layer with minimal operational friction.
Each issuer enters the system with three defined capabilities: the ability to structure participation opportunities, the authority to verify contribution, and the capacity to align participation outcomes with organizational metrics. These capabilities allow issuers to translate existing program goals into participation pathways without redesigning their internal operations.
This design choice is intentional. The pilot is not asking issuers to adopt a new program model; it is enabling them to extend existing programs through coordinated civic capacity. When onboarding feels additive rather than disruptive, institutional participation scales more readily.
In practice, issuer onboarding follows a standardized sequence. Organizations define participation opportunities aligned with their operational objectives. Verification mechanisms are established using issuer-appropriate processes. Issuance parameters are configured to reflect the scope and value of participation. Once live, issuers gain access to participation analytics that allow them to observe engagement patterns and adjust offerings accordingly.
Crucially, the onboarding framework is designed to surface immediate value to issuers. Increased participation, improved visibility into engagement, and expanded access to civic capacity provide tangible benefits that justify continued involvement. As issuers observe measurable improvements in program outcomes, their incentive to deepen integration increases.
Issuer onboarding also serves a signaling function within the pilot. Each additional organization that integrates into the coordination layer increases its perceived legitimacy and utility. For observing institutions, a growing issuer ecosystem reduces uncertainty by demonstrating that participation is not dependent on a single anchor organization.
Over time, this dynamic produces issuer clustering. As organizations recognize shared participant bases and complementary objectives, coordination opportunities expand laterally. The system begins to function less as a collection of individual programs and more as a shared participation substrate.
The pilot therefore tracks issuer engagement not simply as a metric, but as an indicator of infrastructural health. Repeat participation, expansion of participation offerings, and cross-issuer collaboration signal that the coordination layer is embedding into institutional planning processes.
By formalizing issuer onboarding as infrastructure, the City/Sync Pilot ensures that participation pathways are not transient or personality-driven. They are procedural, reproducible, and capable of scaling across organizational boundaries.
This approach directly supports the pilot’s broader objective: establishing programmable coordination as an administrative capability rather than a temporary initiative.
Redemption Architecture
Redemption is the mechanism through which civic participation becomes materially legible within public and nonprofit service ecosystems. For programmable coordination to function as an administrative layer, participation must translate into access pathways that institutions can support without introducing fiscal ambiguity or governance risk.
The City/Sync Pilot approaches redemption as structured service access rather than as financial exchange. Participation credits are designed to operate within clearly bounded redemption environments where the relationship between contribution and access remains operationally transparent.
When redemption is framed as service access, it aligns naturally with existing public-sector practices such as program eligibility, subsidized services, and community benefit structures. Institutions are therefore able to integrate redemption without reclassifying financial obligations or introducing novel budget categories.
Redemption pathways are established in partnership with approved service providers, including nonprofit organizations, public agencies, and community institutions capable of absorbing participation-based access within their existing operational capacity. Wherever possible, the pilot prioritizes redemption models that leverage underutilized inventory, marginal service capacity, or programs designed to expand community reach. This approach allows participation to unlock real value without creating unsustainable demand on institutional resources.
Equally important is political legibility. Observers including policymakers and the public must be able to understand redemption without requiring specialized interpretation. When participation leads to recognizable forms of access such as transit support, educational programming, community services, or recreational opportunities, the system remains intuitively defensible.
The framework also establishes governance boundaries around redemption expansion. New pathways are introduced deliberately rather than reactively, ensuring that growth reflects institutional readiness rather than short-term participation pressure. This measured approach reinforces administrative confidence by demonstrating that the coordination layer can scale without destabilizing service environments.
From a behavioral perspective, redemption completes the participation loop. Contribution becomes consequential when it leads to observable outcomes. Participants recognize that their effort carries tangible impact, while issuers and service providers observe increased engagement across programs. This reciprocity strengthens the coordination layer without requiring speculative incentives.
The pilot therefore evaluates redemption not only through utilization metrics, but through institutional comfort. When service providers continue participation and when expansion occurs without governance friction, redemption can be understood as administratively integrated.
By designing redemption as structured access rather than transactional reward, the City/Sync Pilot preserves alignment with public-sector operating logic while enabling participation to function as a credible component of service ecosystems.

Designing for Replication
The long-term significance of the City/Sync Pilot lies in its operational performance and its capacity to be reproduced across jurisdictions. Administrative innovations achieve systemic impact only when they establish patterns that other institutions can confidently adopt without requiring exceptional conditions.
For this reason, the Pilot Framework is designed from inception with replication as a primary constraint rather than as a downstream ambition.
Replicable models share several characteristics. They integrate with existing governance structures, operate within familiar budgetary logic, and avoid dependencies on uniquely favorable political or organizational circumstances. The City/Sync Pilot adheres to these principles by introducing programmable coordination through procedural pathways that most cities already possess in the form of nonprofit partnerships, service programs, community initiatives, and cross-sector collaboration.
This setup provides operational clarity. Observing jurisdictions must be able to identify the sequence through which deployment occurs, and observe how municipal onboarding to issuer integration, redemption pathway development, and governance oversight operate in sync with one another. When this sequence is legible, adoption shifts from something speculative to something actionable.
The framework documents both outcomes and processes. Participating cities generate administrative artifacts with onboarding protocols, issuer agreements, verification models, redemption structures, and governance procedures, all of which form an implementation blueprint. Over time, this blueprint reduces the informational burden for future adopters, allowing jurisdictions to proceed with greater institutional confidence.
Replication also depends on perceived survivability. Cities are more likely to adopt coordination models that demonstrate stability under normal operating conditions rather than models that rely on extraordinary leadership or temporary funding sources. By embedding the pilot within existing institutional workflows, the framework signals that programmable coordination can persist beyond the tenure of individual champions.
Another critical dimension is adaptability. While the coordination layer maintains structural consistency, its deployment is intentionally modular. Cities retain discretion over issuer composition, participation focus areas, and redemption pathways, enabling alignment with local policy priorities without altering the underlying architecture.
This balance between standardization and local flexibility is essential. Replication requires a stable core accompanied by configurable edges.
As additional jurisdictions evaluate the pilot, replication begins to generate its own momentum. Each successful deployment reduces the perceived novelty of the coordination layer and increases its administrative familiarity. What initially appears innovative gradually becomes expected.
When cities begin planning around the availability of programmable coordination (rather than merely observing it), the layer moves from experimental territory into the domain of operational infrastructure.
The City/Sync Pilot is structured to establish a working and repeatable administrative pattern. Its ultimate success is measured by whether observing jurisdictions conclude that the implementation is both achievable and aligned with their institutional responsibilities.
Governance of the Pilot
The introduction of a new coordination layer into public administration requires governance structures that are both visible and credible. Institutional adoption depends not only on operational feasibility, but on the assurance that oversight mechanisms are capable of guiding the system responsibly as it evolves.
The City/Sync Pilot is governed through a framework that emphasizes parameter control, and administrative transparency. Governance is not an auxiliary function.  It should be embedded directly into the deployment model.
Participating jurisdictions retain policy authority throughout the pilot. Decisions regarding issuer eligibility, redemption expansion, participation focus areas, and operational adjustments remain within institutional discretion. This structure ensures that programmable coordination operates as an extension of public governance rather than as an external influence upon it.
Oversight is reinforced through defined administrative roles responsible for monitoring system behavior, reviewing participation patterns, and authorizing material changes. These roles create clear lines of responsibility, allowing the coordination layer to evolve without introducing ambiguity around decision rights.
Equally important is the principle of bounded adaptability. While the pilot is designed to generate learning, adjustments occur through deliberate governance processes rather than reactive modification. Parameter changes to issuance structures, verification practices, or redemption pathways, all follow review protocols that balance responsiveness with institutional stability.
Transparency further supports governance confidence. Participating institutions maintain visibility into participation flows, issuer activity, and redemption utilization, enabling informed oversight grounded in observable data. This visibility transforms governance from periodic evaluation into continuous administrative awareness.
The framework also anticipates the importance of procedural memory. Documentation of governance decisions, onboarding processes, and operational adjustments ensures that knowledge persists beyond individual administrators. In doing so, the pilot guards against a common failure mode in civic innovation where reliance on singular champions whose departure can destabilize otherwise promising systems.
Governance is structured to scale alongside the coordination layer. Early deployments operate with tighter oversight appropriate to emerging infrastructure. As institutional familiarity increases and operational patterns stabilize, governance can expand proportionally while preserving accountability.
By demonstrating that programmable coordination can function within established governance norms, the pilot reinforces that administrative evolution is durable when it strengthens institutional control rather than diffusing it.
What Success Looks Like
The success of the City/Sync Pilot will be evaluated through observable administrative outcomes that signal the coordination layer is functioning as intended within real institutional environments.
One primary marker of success is municipal operability. Participating jurisdictions must demonstrate that onboarding, issuer integration, redemption pathways, and governance oversight can occur within existing administrative workflows. When coordination infrastructure integrates without requiring structural exception, it signals institutional compatibility.
A second indicator is issuer persistence. Organizations that continue participation beyond initial deployment, by expanding opportunities, refining verification practices, and deepening their integration will provide evidence that the coordination layer delivers operational value. Repeat engagement suggests that issuers have incorporated participation pathways into their programmatic expectations.
Ecosystem expansion is also an important category for success. As additional nonprofits, agencies, and service providers seek integration, the coordination layer begins to exhibit gravitational pull. Growth driven by institutional interest rather than centralized recruitment indicates that the model is generating its own momentum.
Participation stability offers another meaningful signal. Success is not defined by short-term surges in engagement, but by the emergence of predictable contribution patterns. When participation becomes steady rather than episodic, institutions gain the confidence necessary to plan around civic capacity.
Redemption continuity further reinforces administrative viability. Service providers that maintain redemption pathways without experiencing operational strain demonstrate that participation-based access can coexist with service delivery obligations. Institutional comfort is as significant as utilization volume.
Governance confidence serves as an overarching measure as well. When policymakers and administrators regard the coordination layer as observable and controllable, the system transitions from perceived innovation to accepted capability.
The pilot succeeds when programmable coordination begins to feel less like a distinct initiative and more like an expected component of public service environments.
At that point, the most important outcome emerges. Observing jurisdictions no longer evaluate whether such a model is possible. Instead, they begin assessing when and how it might be appropriate within their own administrative contexts.
Success is defined by the pilot’s ability to shift the posture of other institutions, from curiosity toward consideration.
The Direction of Public Coordination
Administrative systems rarely transform through singular events. More often, change becomes visible when new operating patterns begin to function within the routines of governance. Over time, what initially appears novel is gradually absorbed into institutional expectation.
The City/Sync Pilot is structured to contribute to such a transition. By introducing programmable coordination through governed deployment rather than advocacy, the framework allows institutions to observe how a new administrative layer can operate alongside established structures.
When cities demonstrate the ability to onboard into participation infrastructure, integrate issuer ecosystems, support redemption pathways, and govern the system within existing oversight norms, programmable coordination begins to appear more administratively attainable.
Public institutions rarely adopt models that feel speculative. They adopt patterns that have been made operational elsewhere.
If the framework succeeds in establishing these patterns, its long-term impact will extend beyond the participating jurisdictions. It will have helped define a procedural pathway through which a new coordination layer can enter public administration.
And as with many durable governance patterns, what begins as structured deployment may, over time, come to be regarded simply as part of how cities operate.
