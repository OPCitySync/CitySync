City/Sync Money Transmission Risk Checklist

Prepared for: City/Sync internal planning and pilot design review

Date: March 26, 2026

Important: This document is not legal advice and should not be treated as a formal legal opinion. It is a practical architecture and risk-positioning memo designed to help City/Sync maintain a strong regulatory posture while preparing the Pilot Program. Formal deployment decisions should still be reviewed by qualified counsel in the relevant jurisdictions.

Working Conclusion

Based on the current pilot architecture, City/Sync does not appear to be clearly operating as a money transmitter under the ordinary federal framework used by FinCEN, because the system is structured as a non-transferable, earned, closed-loop civic credit rather than a business that accepts and transmits money or convertible value on behalf of users.

That conclusion depends heavily on the system continuing to operate as designed. The stronger the protocol remains as a bounded civic-credit model, the stronger the regulatory position. The more it begins to resemble open-loop payments, stored value, or cash conversion, the weaker that position becomes.

Why the Current Architecture Is Favorable

  • CITY is earned, not purchased. Credits are minted after verified civic contribution rather than sold for fiat or crypto.
  • CITY is non-transferable. Users cannot send it peer-to-peer or use it as a freely circulating payment instrument.
  • CITY is not cash redeemable. It is spent on defined offerings inside the pilot rather than converted back into money.
  • Redemption burns the credit. The token lifecycle is tied to contribution and access, not monetary settlement between users.
  • City/Sync is not a custodial remittance layer. The platform is not intended to receive funds from one party and transmit them to another party as part of its core model.
  • The network is bounded. Redeemer participation, offerings, and governance are all restricted by program rules rather than open-market usage.

Green / Yellow / Red Checklist

Green — Features That Strongly Support the Current Position

  • CITY remains non-transferable at the contract level, not merely hidden in the user interface.
  • No one can buy CITY with fiat, stablecoins, or other cryptoassets.
  • No user can cash out CITY for dollars, USDC, or other monetary value.
  • Participants redeem CITY only for specific goods or services offered by approved Redeemer Organizations.
  • Redemptions burn CITY rather than route value onward to another user.
  • City/Sync does not hold customer balances for later transfer.
  • The protocol and all public-facing materials consistently describe CITY as a civic-program credit or access instrument, not as money.
  • Redeemer relationships are documented as program participation arrangements, not payment processing arrangements.

Yellow — Features That Create Legal Ambiguity and Need Counsel Review

  • Redeemers are reimbursed off-chain in a way that starts to resemble settlement of monetary obligations through the platform.
  • Credits begin to function as a de facto general-purpose medium of exchange within a large merchant network.
  • Users can indirectly transfer value by using CITY to purchase transferrable or resellable benefits.
  • Third-party integrations create a practical market price for CITY, even if City/Sync itself does not sell it.
  • The protocol allows broad interoperability before legal treatment is mapped jurisdiction by jurisdiction.
  • Public communications begin describing CITY as equivalent to cash, spending power, compensation, or wages.

Red — Design Choices That Would Materially Increase Money-Transmission Risk

  • Users can transfer CITY to each other.
  • Users can swap CITY for fiat, USDC, or other crypto.
  • City/Sync or an affiliate offers to buy back or redeem CITY for money.
  • City/Sync accepts customer funds and settles value onward to participants or redeemers.
  • CITY becomes a widely usable merchant token outside the bounded program environment.
  • Wrapped, bridged, or synthetic versions of CITY are allowed to circulate in secondary markets.

What Must Stay True for the Strongest Position

  1. CITY must remain earned rather than sold.
  2. CITY must remain non-transferable.
  3. CITY must remain non-cash redeemable.
  4. The pilot must remain a closed, governed, role-based network.
  5. City/Sync must avoid becoming a custodian of customer monetary value.
  6. Redeemer access should remain tied to specific offerings and program rules.
  7. All contracts, terms, accounting treatment, and public messaging should reinforce that CITY is a limited civic credit, not a general monetary instrument.
  8. Any expansion in scope should be reviewed before launch for both federal and state implications.

Why State Law Still Matters

Even if the federal position is reasonably favorable, state law may be broader or more aggressive. That is especially true in jurisdictions with distinct virtual-currency or money-transmission regimes. The practical takeaway is simple: the current architecture gives City/Sync a stronger federal argument, but it does not eliminate the need for state-level analysis before a scaled deployment.

Recommended Pilot Guardrails

  • Keep the smart-contract restrictions that prevent transfer and approval.
  • Do not introduce any fiat purchase flow for CITY.
  • Do not introduce any redemption-to-cash or stablecoin path.
  • Prohibit secondary-market activity involving CITY in the terms of service and pilot agreements.
  • Keep redeemer offerings narrowly defined and program-bound.
  • Review any off-chain reimbursement or subsidy structure so it does not convert the platform into a settlement business.
  • Maintain clear written descriptions of CITY as a civic credit, access instrument, and governance-controlled program unit.
  • Conduct jurisdiction-specific legal review before moving from bounded pilot to multi-city deployment.

Questions Counsel Should Eventually Answer

  • Does any state treat a non-transferable civic-program credit as regulated money transmission or stored value under its own statutes?
  • Would any off-chain reimbursement to redeemers change the legal analysis?
  • Should the pilot rely on additional contractual restrictions to reinforce the non-monetary nature of CITY?
  • Are there labor, tax, consumer protection, or prepaid access issues that should be analyzed separately from money transmission?
  • What deployment changes would require a new legal review before launch?

Bottom Line

City/Sync's strongest legal position is that CITY functions as a non-transferable, earned, closed-loop civic credit used to access defined offerings inside a governed local program. On that architecture, the system does not look like a conventional money transmission business. The main legal risk is not the current design itself. The main legal risk is drift: adding transferability, convertibility, custody, or open-loop payment features that make the system behave more like regulated financial infrastructure.

Background sources considered for this internal summary include FinCEN guidance on virtual currency and the current City/Sync pilot architecture.
